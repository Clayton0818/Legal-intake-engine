# Data Privacy & Retention Policy — Research Draft

**Board card:** `c2` — Data privacy & retention policy
**Status:** Research and recommended approach. **Not a legal opinion.** This document must be reviewed and approved by a licensed attorney (ideally one qualified in data privacy/cybersecurity law, working alongside the UPL/ethics reviewer for card `c26`) before any of it is relied on operationally or published to customers.
**Scope note:** Target launch states are not yet finalized (blocked on founder card `c28`). This policy is written to be defensible across the current landscape of U.S. state comprehensive privacy laws, with Texas called out specifically as a worked example, since `c1` (UPL review) was narrowed to Texas at the founder's request and Texas is the most concrete jurisdiction currently in the repo. Every dollar figure, deadline, and threshold below should be re-checked against the specific states chosen before this becomes firm policy — privacy law is one of the fastest-moving areas of U.S. regulation and this draft will go stale.

## 1. What this product actually handles

Before picking a compliance framework, it's worth being precise about what data a "legal intake AI" collects, because the answer determines which privacy regimes apply and how sensitive the retention question is.

A conversational intake flow (see card `c12`) will typically capture, per inquiry:

- **Direct identifiers**: name, phone, email, mailing address, sometimes date of birth.
- **Matter narrative**: free-text description of the legal problem — this is often where the sensitive data hides, because a prospective client describing "my situation" in their own words will frequently volunteer health details, immigration status, criminal history, family relationships, financial information, or details about a minor, regardless of what the intake form technically asks for.
- **Practice-area-specific structured fields**: e.g., accident date and injuries (personal injury), immigration status and country of origin (immigration), employer and income (employment/family law), charges and custody status (criminal defense).
- **Conflict-check inputs**: names of adverse parties, related entities — data about *third parties who are not the person filling out the form*, which raises separate handling questions (see §7).
- **Metadata**: IP address, device/browser info, timestamps, referral source, and — once the LLM triage classifier (`c13`) exists — the model's inferred practice area and urgency classification for that inquiry.

Several of these fall inside statutory "sensitive data" categories in every state law surveyed below (health condition, precise geolocation in some cases, citizenship/immigration status, and — for family law or any intake involving a client's children — data about minors). That places this product's baseline compliance bar closer to the "sensitive data" tier than a generic marketing-lead-capture tool, even before a single state is chosen. The policy should be written assuming sensitive data will routinely be present in intake records, not as an edge case.

There is also a structural point specific to a legal intake product: a meaningful share of the "personal data" being processed belongs to people who are **not** the company's contracting customer (the law firm) and **not yet a client of that firm** — they are prospective clients, and in the conflict-check case, third parties who never interacted with the product at all. Consumer privacy statutes are generally written around "consumers," a term that in most of these laws just means a natural person acting in an individual capacity — prospective clients qualify. This needs to be confirmed jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction (some state laws carve out certain employment- or B2B-context data, which is not the situation here).

## 2. Two overlapping regulatory layers

This is the important structural point for the whole document: **two different bodies of law govern the same data simultaneously**, and they don't always point the same direction.

1. **State consumer/comprehensive privacy statutes** (CCPA/CPRA, TDPSA, VCDPA, and ~17 others) — these regulate the *company* (the intake-engine vendor, and separately each subscribing law firm) as a data controller or processor, and give the *data subject* (the prospective client) statutory rights over their own data: access, correction, deletion, portability, opt-out of sale/targeted advertising/certain profiling.
2. **Attorney professional-responsibility rules** (ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct as adopted state-by-state, principally Rule 1.6 confidentiality, Rule 1.1 competence, and Rule 1.15 safekeeping) plus the **attorney-client privilege and work-product doctrine** — these govern the *law firm's* and, derivatively, the *firm's vendors'* handling of client confidences, and they generally impose a **stricter, not looser**, standard than consumer privacy law. Deleting data because a consumer privacy statute nominally allows it can conflict with a firm's separate legal obligation to retain client files (state bar file-retention rules, litigation holds, statute-of-limitations considerations, malpractice-defense needs).

The retention schedule and deletion-request workflow in §6–7 below exist specifically to reconcile these two layers — the design principle is "consumer privacy rights, subject to a legal-hold/ethics-rule override," not "whichever request comes in last wins."

## 3. State consumer privacy law landscape (current as of September 2026)

As of this writing, twenty states have enacted comprehensive consumer privacy laws (counting Florida's narrower Digital Bill of Rights), per MultiState's 2026 tracker. Effective dates relevant to this product:

**Already in effect:** California (CCPA/CPRA), Virginia (VCDPA), Colorado (CPA), Connecticut (CTDPA), Utah (UCPA), Texas (TDPSA — effective July 1, 2024), Oregon, Montana, Iowa, Delaware, New Jersey, New Hampshire, Nebraska, Minnesota, Maryland, and others enacted in the 2023–2025 wave.

**Newly effective in 2026:** Indiana (Jan 1, 2026), Kentucky (Jan 1, 2026), Rhode Island (Jan 1, 2026); Connecticut, Arkansas, and Utah have amendments or additional provisions taking effect July 1, 2026. California added consumer health data provisions (AB 45) and data-broker registration deadlines effective in 2026.

**Recommended approach given no target states are locked yet:** rather than building to the lowest common denominator (Utah, which is the lightest-touch of the batch) or waiting for `c28`, this policy assumes something close to **CCPA/CPRA-level obligations as the design ceiling** (California is both the strictest widely-adopted regime and the one most likely to apply regardless of launch-state choice, since CCPA applies based on where the *consumer* resides, not where the *business* is headquartered — any California resident who uses a subscribing firm's intake widget triggers CCPA regardless of which state the firm operates in or which states the founder "targets"). Practically: if the product is built CCPA-compliant, it is very likely compliant or close to compliant with the lighter state laws too. This is a recommendation, not a settled decision — it should be revisited once `c28` narrows the launch states, since a Utah- or Texas-only rollout with contractual restrictions preventing use by California-resident data subjects could justify a lighter build. That contractual restriction itself would need its own review.

**Texas specifics (TDPSA), as the current worked example:** effective July 1, 2024; applies to any entity conducting business in Texas or producing a product/service consumed by Texas residents that processes personal data (no minimum-revenue or data-volume threshold, unlike California — Texas exempts only entities that qualify as "small businesses" under the federal SBA standard, except when selling sensitive data, which this product does not do). TDPSA's definition of "sensitive data" explicitly includes racial/ethnic origin, religious beliefs, health condition/diagnosis, sexuality, citizenship/immigration status, genetic/biometric identifying data, and precise geolocation — several of which will appear routinely in intake narratives per §1. TDPSA requires a data protection assessment before engaging in processing that presents a heightened risk to consumers, which almost certainly includes processing sensitive data at scale, meaning the intake product likely needs one. Enforcement is exclusively by the Texas Attorney General with a 30-day cure period and penalties up to $7,500 per violation; there is no private right of action. Consumers get access, correction, deletion, and opt-out rights, with a 45-day response window (extendable to 90).

**Open question for attorney review:** whether the intake-engine company should be structured, in most target states, as the "processor" (Texas/Virginia/Colorado terminology) or "service provider" (California terminology) acting on the *subscribing law firm's* instructions — with the **firm as controller/business** — or whether in some flows (e.g., before a firm relationship attaches, during pure lead-routing) the company itself becomes a controller. This affects who owes consumers a privacy notice, who can be the target of a deletion request, and what has to be in the firm-facing contract (a data processing addendum, "DPA," between the company and each subscribing firm is very likely required either way — see §8).

## 4. Professional-responsibility overlay

This layer is arguably the more consequential one for a legal-vertical product and is easy to under-weight if the team defaults to generic SaaS privacy practices.

**ABA Model Rule 1.6 (Confidentiality)** and its state analogs prohibit a lawyer from revealing information relating to representation of a client without informed consent, with an affirmative duty under Rule 1.6(c) (added in the 2012 Ethics 20/20 amendments and now widely adopted) to make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access to or disclosure of client information. State-bar cloud-computing ethics opinions (building on the reasoning of, e.g., ABA Formal Opinion 477R on transmitting confidential information) generally permit cloud storage and SaaS vendors so long as the lawyer/firm exercises reasonable care in vendor selection — meaning firms subscribing to this product will be relying on **this company's** security and confidentiality representations to satisfy their own Rule 1.6 obligations. That makes the vendor's contractual and technical commitments (encryption, access control, breach notification timelines, subprocessor disclosure) load-bearing for every subscribing firm's own ethics compliance, not just this company's.

**ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024)**, the first ABA ethics opinion specifically on generative AI, is directly on point for a product whose triage classifier and (eventually) conversational flow are LLM-driven:

- Under **Rule 1.6**, lawyers must understand, at least at a functional level, how a GAI tool processes and retains data, and must implement safeguards against unauthorized disclosure before inputting client information into it.
- The opinion is explicit that **standard engagement-letter boilerplate is not sufficient informed consent** for using client confidences in a GAI tool — consent needs to be specific enough that the client understands their information may be processed by an AI system, ideally naming the practice and what it means in plain terms.
- Under **Rule 1.1 (Competence)**, lawyers have an ongoing duty to understand the capabilities and limitations of the GAI tools they use — a duty that, practically, this product should make easy to satisfy rather than something firms have to independently research (e.g., plain-language documentation of what the triage classifier does and doesn't do, and its known failure modes).
- The opinion also flags risk when multiple lawyers/firms share the same GAI platform — relevant here because this is explicitly a **multi-tenant** product (see card `c8`): the architecture needs to make cross-tenant data leakage into a shared model context structurally impossible, not just a policy promise, because Opinion 512's concern is exactly the scenario of one firm's client data becoming visible or influential to another firm's session.

**Practical implication for this policy:** because subscribing firms will need to give their own clients a Rule-1.6-compliant, specific-enough AI disclosure (per Opinion 512) before those clients' information flows into this product, the intake-engine company should provide **model consent/disclosure language** that firms can adapt, rather than leaving each subscribing firm to draft this alone — this belongs in the terms-of-service/liability-disclaimers work (`c10`) and possibly in a customer-facing "how we use AI" page, informed by this document. Flagging this as an explicit hand-off to `c10`.

## 5. Attorney-client privilege and work-product considerations

Separate from confidentiality-as-an-ethics-duty, there is the evidentiary question of whether routing a prospective client's communications through a third-party AI intake product could support an argument that privilege was waived (disclosure to a third party outside the privileged relationship is a classic privilege-waiver trigger) or that work product protection doesn't attach.

Current commentary in this area (2025–2026) generally converges on a few points, though the law is genuinely unsettled and there is limited case law directly on point for AI intake tools specifically:

- Privilege is more likely to survive where the vendor is acting purely as the *functional equivalent of the firm's own systems* (i.e., a processor bound by a confidentiality/DPA agreement, not disclosing data to unrelated third parties, not using client data to train models shared across customers) — this reinforces the processor/service-provider framing from §3 and should shape the vendor agreement and any LLM API vendor selection (see §8's note on model-provider opt-outs).
- Privilege attaches to communications made **for the purpose of obtaining legal advice** within an existing or forming attorney-client relationship. A pre-engagement intake conversation is in a genuine gray zone: many jurisdictions extend privilege to prospective-client communications made for the purpose of seeking representation (see, e.g., ABA Model Rule 1.18 on duties to prospective clients, which assumes some confidentiality attaches even before engagement), but this is jurisdiction-dependent and not guaranteed, especially for inquiries that never result in engagement.
- Because the classifier and any future features process the *content* of the intake narrative, not just metadata, the product should be built assuming everything a prospective client types could later be treated as privileged/confidential material, and the security posture (`c9`) should not distinguish between "still just a lead" and "now a client" — the moment of engagement is often unclear in real time and retroactively drawing the line is not workable.

**This is squarely a topic for the attorney reviewer**, not something this document can resolve — flagging as an open question in §9.

## 6. Recommended retention schedule

The core tension: consumer privacy law generally favors data minimization and honoring deletion requests promptly; legal-practice reality favors retaining client-file-adjacent records for years (state bar file-retention rules commonly range 5–10 years post-matter-closure, malpractice statutes of limitations plus discovery-rule tails can extend further, and litigation holds override everything). The recommended design:

| Data category | Recommended retention | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Declined / non-engaged inquiries** (no firm-client relationship ever formed) | 90 days active, then either delete or de-identify/aggregate for the firm's own analytics, unless the firm's own policy or applicable conflicts-checking need requires longer | These are the records with the weakest retention justification and the strongest privacy-minimization argument; also the category most likely to be a pure consumer-privacy-law deletion request with no competing ethics obligation. |
| **Conflict-check records** (names/entities checked, including for people who were never themselves an intake subject) | Retained per the subscribing firm's own conflicts-database policy, typically indefinitely, but stored as minimally as possible (name + matter-adverse-party flag, not full narrative) | Conflicts data has a permanent legitimate purpose (preventing future conflicts) independent of any one matter's lifecycle — this is a case where indefinite retention is the *defensible* choice, not the risky one, but the data captured should be narrowly scoped. |
| **Active-matter intake data** (firm has engaged the prospective client) | Retained for the life of the matter plus the firm's standard post-closure file-retention period (commonly 5–10 years, state-bar and malpractice-insurer dependent — this is a per-firm, per-jurisdiction figure the product should let firms configure, not hardcode) | This data becomes part of the client file; the underlying record-retention obligation is the firm's, under its bar's rules, not a product design choice. |
| **AI processing logs / classifier outputs** (model inputs, outputs, confidence scores tied to a given inquiry) | Should not outlive the underlying intake record; recommend a materially *shorter* default (e.g., 30–90 days) for raw model I/O used for debugging/QA purposes specifically, separate from the intake record itself, unless there's a specific documented purpose (e.g., ongoing malpractice-defense need, active dispute) to keep it longer | Model I/O logs are the highest-risk category to retain indefinitely — they are rarely essential to the client file itself, are attractive breach targets, and their necessity decays fastest. |
| **Data belonging to a subject who submits a verified deletion request** | Delete/anonymize on the timeline required by applicable law (see §7), **except** any subset that must be preserved for an active legal hold, an active or reasonably anticipated dispute, or a firm's independent record-retention obligation — document the specific legal basis for any such exception in the deletion-request record itself | This is the point where §2's two-layer tension gets resolved case-by-case; a blanket "we never delete because we might need it" policy will not survive attorney-general scrutiny under any of the state statutes surveyed, and a blanket "we always delete on request" policy will not survive a firm's own bar obligations. |

A hard technical requirement that falls out of this table: the system needs a **legal-hold flag** at the matter/inquiry level that can override a deletion request, with the override reason recorded — this should be treated as a requirement for the core data model (`c19`), not an afterthought bolted on later.

## 7. Data subject rights workflow

Recommended handling for access/correction/deletion requests, reconciling consumer-privacy rights with the legal-hold override from §6:

1. **Intake** — request comes in either to the subscribing firm directly or, if the product exposes a self-service mechanism, to the company. Recommend routing all such requests to the *firm* by default, since the firm is very likely the controller/business under §3, with the company (as processor/service provider) obligated by contract to assist within a defined SLA (most state laws require the business to substantively respond within 45 days, so the company's internal SLA to the firm needs meaningful headroom inside that window — recommend 15 business days).
2. **Legal-hold / privilege check** — before fulfilling a deletion request specifically, check the matter/inquiry-level hold flag from §6. If flagged, the request is not silently ignored — the firm (as the party with the actual attorney-client relationship and legal judgment) makes the call on how to respond, informed by the specific reason recorded. The product should surface this decision point to the firm, not make it automatically.
3. **Third-party subjects** (conflict-check adverse parties, people mentioned in a narrative but who are not the submitting individual) — these people are very unlikely to know this product exists and have no direct relationship to invoke rights against. Recommend the position that the *submitting party's* consent/disclosure covers the firm's own permissible use of that information for conflicts purposes, consistent with how conflicts databases have always operated, but this is exactly the kind of edge case an attorney should bless explicitly rather than the product assuming it away.
4. **Authentication of the requester** — before fulfilling any access or deletion request, verify the requester is who they claim to be, at a level of assurance proportionate to the sensitivity of the data (this is a common statutory requirement, e.g., under CCPA regulations) and ties into the access-control work in `c9`.

## 8. Third-party processors and AI model vendors

Two vendor categories need explicit data-handling terms, and neither should be treated as a generic SaaS procurement decision given everything in §4–5:

- **LLM/model API providers** (whichever vendor the eventual stack decision, `c18`, selects for the triage classifier and conversational flow) — the contract needs to confirm the provider does **not** train on submitted data by default, or that training is contractually disabled/opted out; that data is encrypted in transit and, ideally, not retained by the provider beyond what's needed to serve the request (or retained only under a defined, short window with clear deletion); and that the provider will sign a data processing agreement suitable for potentially privileged legal content. This should be a hard selection criterion in `c18`, not a follow-up.
- **Every subprocessor a subscribing firm's data touches** (hosting, model providers, analytics, error-monitoring/logging tools, etc.) should be disclosed to firms in a maintained subprocessor list, consistent with how CCPA/CPRA and most state laws expect "service providers" to flow obligations down their own subprocessor chain, and consistent with the ethics obligation in §4 that firms are relying on this company's vendor diligence to satisfy their own Rule 1.6 duties.

A **data processing addendum (DPA)** between the company and each subscribing firm — defining roles (§3's open question), permitted purposes, subprocessor flow-down, breach notification timelines, and deletion obligations — is very likely a required contractual artifact regardless of which target states are chosen. Recommend this be drafted alongside the terms of service (`c10`), not treated as a separate later task, since the two documents need to be internally consistent.

## 9. Breach notification

Every U.S. state (all 50, plus D.C. and the territories) has its own data breach notification statute, triggered by unauthorized acquisition of unencrypted personal information, with varying notification deadlines (commonly "without unreasonable delay," with several states now specifying hard outer limits — Texas, for example, requires notification within 60 days of determining a breach occurred, and requires notice to the Texas Attorney General within 60 days if 250+ Texas residents are affected). Given the product will likely operate across multiple states from day one, the recommended internal target is the **strictest applicable deadline across all states the firm's clients could plausibly reside in**, rather than maintaining a matrix of fifty different clocks — operationally simpler and defensible. This needs firming up against the actual target-state list once `c28` resolves, and the specific deadline figures above should be re-verified at that time since breach-notification statutes are amended more often than they're repealed.

## 10. Summary of open questions for attorney review

1. Controller-vs-processor role allocation between the company and subscribing firms (§3) — this determines who owes privacy notices and who is the proper target of consumer rights requests, and should be settled before the DPA (§8) is drafted.
2. Whether/how attorney-client privilege attaches to pre-engagement intake conversations in the actual target jurisdictions, and what product design choices (if any) would strengthen a privilege claim versus weaken it (§5).
3. Whether pure consumer-privacy-law deletion rights can be validly limited by a firm's independent bar-imposed file-retention obligation, and how to document that override defensibly at scale (§6–7).
4. Whether the CCPA-level "design ceiling" recommended in §3 is the right call, or whether a narrower initial state footprint (once `c28` resolves) justifies a lighter build — and whether restricting the product from California residents is itself workable or advisable.
5. Model consent/disclosure language for firms to give their own clients about AI processing, satisfying ABA Formal Opinion 512 — a joint deliverable with `c10` (§4).
6. Whether a HIPAA Business Associate Agreement is ever required — flagged as out of scope for this draft, but worth an attorney's quick read: personal-injury and workers'-comp intake in particular will routinely capture health information, and if a subscribing firm or a downstream party (e.g., a referred medical provider) is itself a HIPAA covered entity, that could pull this product into BAA territory in specific workflows even though the company itself is not a covered entity.
