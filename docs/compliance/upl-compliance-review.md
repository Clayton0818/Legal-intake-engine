## 1. Why this matters and what's actually at risk

The product's core function — a conversational AI that takes in a prospective client's facts, sorts them by practice area and urgency, and helps a firm decide whether to take the matter — sits directly on top of the line between "legal information" (generally fine for anyone to provide) and "legal advice" (restricted to licensed attorneys). Texas has a statutory UPL definition, a standing enforcement committee, criminal penalties for some categories of violation, and — distinctively — a separate solicitation/barratry statute and a separate lawyer-referral-service licensing scheme that both bear directly on how an intake-and-routing product can operate. On top of the state-law picture, the FTC's 2024–2025 action against DoNotPay showed that federal consumer-protection law is an independent and live source of exposure for AI legal products, regardless of what any state bar does.

Two distinct risk surfaces exist for this product, and they should not be conflated:

1. **Risk to the deploying law firm.** If a Texas firm licenses this product and its own staff/attorneys use it as an intake tool (the primary go-to-market model per `docs/product`), the firm is a licensed entity directing a tool the way it might direct a paralegal or an answering service. This is the lower-risk posture, analogous to existing legal-intake software (Clio Grow, Lawmatics, Intaker) and answering services (Smith.ai) that already operate in this space without having been found to constitute UPL, provided the tool doesn't independently generate legal advice or legal conclusions that the firm passes along unreviewed. Texas Disciplinary Rule of Professional Conduct 5.5(b) separately prohibits a lawyer from assisting a non-lawyer in UPL, which puts some of the burden on the deploying firm's own attorneys to supervise the tool properly — worth flagging in the firm-facing terms of service (`c10`).
2. **Risk to us as the vendor, if the product (or a future version of it) talks to prospective clients in a way that itself looks like practicing law** — e.g., if the AI tells a user "you have a valid claim," "you're within the statute of limitations," "you don't need a lawyer for this," or drafts something like a demand letter or a specific legal document based on their facts. This is the DoNotPay scenario, and it is the scenario the product's language, scripting, and feature set need to be actively designed away from — not just at launch, but as a standing constraint on every future feature that touches user-facing text.

The rest of this document treats risk surface 2 as the one that needs concrete design guardrails, since risk surface 1 is largely a function of how the deploying firm supervises the tool.

## 2. The general legal framework

Texas, like every state, has no bright-line definition of "the practice of law" — the statutory text is broad and non-exhaustive, and Texas courts retain authority to decide that conduct not enumerated in the statute is still UPL. A few concepts recur across the Texas-specific sources below:

- **The core test is "legal advice" vs. "legal information."** Explaining what the law generally says, publishing self-help materials, and providing blank forms or software that a person fills out and interprets themselves is treated as legal information and is not UPL. Applying the law to a specific person's facts and circumstances — telling them what it means for *them* and what they should do — is legal advice, and providing it without a license is UPL.
- **Document assembly and interactive tools are a gray zone, not an automatic safe harbor** — except that Texas is unusual in having carved out a specific statutory exception for software and published materials (Section 3 below), created directly in response to litigation over a legal-document-preparation product. That exception is a genuinely useful anchor for this product's Texas posture, but its boundaries (particularly whether it extends to a conversational, branching AI rather than a static form) have not been tested in court and shouldn't be assumed to cover every feature this product might build.
- **Referral and solicitation of legal business are separately and specifically regulated in Texas**, on top of general UPL rules — Sections 4 and 5 below. This matters more for an intake/triage product than it would in a state that only has a general UPL statute, because two of the product's likely future features (routing inquiries to multiple partner firms, and any form of proactive outreach) map directly onto these Texas-specific regimes.

## 3. The Texas UPL statute and the software/forms exception

Texas's core UPL definition is codified at **Texas Government Code § 81.101**. Subsection (a) defines "practice of law" broadly: preparing pleadings or managing a proceeding on behalf of a client before a court, "as well as a service rendered out of court, including the giving of advice or the rendering of any service requiring the use of legal skill or knowledge, such as preparing a will, contract, or other instrument, the legal effect of which under the facts and conclusions involved must be carefully determined." Subsection (b) makes clear the definition is non-exclusive — Texas courts can find other conduct to be UPL even if it isn't listed.

Subsection (c) is the part most directly relevant to this product, and it exists because of a case study worth understanding in full: in the 1990s, the Texas Unauthorized Practice of Law Committee sued Parsons Technology over its "Quicken Family Lawyer" software, which generated legal documents from user answers. A federal district court sided with the Committee and enjoined the software's sale in Texas, holding that it met the statutory definition of practicing law. Before the Fifth Circuit could rule on the merits, the Texas Legislature intervened directly: House Bill 1507 (1999) amended § 81.101 to add subsection (c), which excludes from the definition of "practice of law" the "design, creation, publication, distribution, display, or sale, including by means of an Internet website, of written materials, books, forms, computer software, or similar products" — **so long as the products clearly and conspicuously state that they are not a substitute for the advice of an attorney**. The Fifth Circuit vacated the injunction and remanded in light of the new statute (*Unauthorized Practice of Law Committee v. Parsons Technology, Inc.*, 179 F.3d 956 (5th Cir. 1999)).

This is the single most important piece of Texas-specific law for this product, and it cuts both ways:

- It's a real, legislatively-created opening for legal-technology products that Texas deliberately created to avoid strangling consumer access to affordable legal tools. A product that behaves like the software the Texas Legislature had in mind — generating information, forms, or document drafts, with a clear and conspicuous "not a substitute for an attorney" disclosure — has a specific statutory argument that it isn't practicing law, not just a general "we're just gathering facts" argument.
- It has never been tested against a conversational, dynamically-branching AI system that actively asks follow-up questions and characterizes what it learns in real time, the way this product's intake flow and triage classifier are meant to work. The exception's text talks about "design, creation, publication, distribution, display, or sale" of "products," which reads naturally as covering the software itself, not necessarily every real-time interaction the software has with a specific user's specific facts. Whether an LLM-driven back-and-forth that adapts its questions to a user's answers still falls inside "software" as contemplated by subsection (c), or has effectively become "the rendering of any service requiring the use of legal skill" under subsection (a) because of how interactive and fact-specific it gets, is an open question this document flags rather than resolves (see Section 7).

**Enforcement.** Texas Government Code §§ 81.103–81.107 establish the Unauthorized Practice of Law Committee (UPLC) — nine members appointed by the Texas Supreme Court, at least three of whom must be non-attorneys, funded through the State Bar's budget. The UPLC investigates complaints (intake is via an online complaint form) and can pursue civil injunctive relief against non-lawyer UPL; licensed Texas attorneys and the State Bar can also sue to stop UPL independently of the Committee. Separately, criminal exposure exists: falsely holding oneself out as a lawyer is a state-jail felony under Texas Penal Code § 38.122, and non-attorney representation in certain matters (e.g., personal-injury or property-damage claims) can be charged as a misdemeanor. No Texas UPLC enforcement action or reported case specifically targeting an AI-driven intake or triage tool was identified in this research pass — the regulatory territory here is largely unoccupied so far, which cuts toward caution rather than comfort, since it means there's no existing Texas precedent to calibrate against either way.

## 4. Barratry and solicitation — Texas Penal Code § 38.12

This statute is not a UPL provision, but it is arguably more directly relevant to an intake product than UPL is, because it specifically regulates *how prospective clients get contacted about legal representation* — which is close to the core function of an intake-and-routing tool, especially if the product's roadmap (per `docs/go-to-market`) ever moves toward proactive outreach rather than purely inbound chat.

Texas Penal Code § 38.12 criminalizes "barratry," which includes soliciting employment (in person or by telephone) for oneself or another with intent to obtain economic benefit, offering or accepting payment to solicit clients, and pursuing claims one isn't authorized to pursue. It layers on additional, specific restrictions for certain professions including attorneys: unsolicited written or electronic communication, or in-person/telephone solicitation, is separately restricted when it relates to a personal injury or wrongful death **within 31 days of the incident**, when the recipient is already represented by counsel, when the recipient has indicated they don't want to be contacted, or when the communication involves coercion, fraud, harassment, or misleading statements. Violations of the general solicitation provisions are a third-degree felony; violations of the profession-specific restrictions are a Class A misdemeanor for a first offense and a third-degree felony for repeat offenses. The statute doesn't specifically mention automated or AI-driven outreach, which means an automated system that initiates contact would most likely be evaluated under the same standards as if a human had sent the message — the "it was an algorithm, not a person" framing is unlikely to be a defense, similar to how New York's proposed chatbot bill (a different state, but the same underlying regulatory instinct) explicitly bars using "it's just an AI" as a shield.

**Practical implication for this product:** as long as the intake flow is purely inbound — a prospective client initiates contact with a firm that already has a relationship with them or that they sought out — barratry exposure is low. The moment any feature involves the product (or a firm using it) reaching out first, especially around a personal-injury event, the 31-day rule and the broader solicitation restrictions become directly relevant and should get a dedicated review before that feature is built, not retrofitted after.

## 5. Lawyer referral services — Texas Occupations Code Chapter 952

If the product's go-to-market model ever evolves from "a tool one firm uses for its own intake" toward "a service that takes in a prospective client's inquiry and routes it to whichever partner firm fits" — i.e., an actual referral function across multiple, otherwise-unrelated firms — Texas regulates that activity separately and specifically as a "lawyer referral service" under Occupations Code Chapter 952, and the eligibility bar is a significant constraint worth flagging early.

Under Chapter 952, an entity operating a lawyer referral service in Texas must hold a state-issued certificate, and — critically — **only a governmental entity or a nonprofit organization exempt under IRC § 501(c)(3), (c)(4), or (c)(6)** may qualify for that certificate; the operator must certify it operates "primarily for the benefit of the public," maintain a roster of at least 25 contracted lawyers from different firms, and comply with fee limits (combined referral-service and attorney fees can't exceed $20 for the first 30 minutes of an initial consultation) and consumer-protection requirements (complaint procedures, lawyer suspension/removal policies, a required certification notice). Enforcement runs through the State Bar of Texas, via injunctive relief and recovery of costs and fees.

**Practical implication:** a for-profit SaaS company cannot itself become a certified Texas lawyer referral service under this framework — the nonprofit/governmental eligibility requirement forecloses that path outright. This is a strong, concrete reason (independent of the general UPL caution already in Section 1) to keep the Texas version of this product scoped as a tool a specific, already-engaged firm uses for its own intake, rather than as a multi-firm matching or routing service, unless a genuinely separate nonprofit referral entity were formed to hold the certificate — which is a materially different corporate and business-model undertaking, not a feature to bolt on later.

## 6. The DoNotPay precedent (why this is not hypothetical)

In September 2024 the FTC brought an enforcement action against DoNotPay for marketing itself as "the world's first robot lawyer" and claiming its AI could generate legal documents "on par with the work of expensive attorneys," without ever testing the tool against actual licensed-attorney work or employing attorneys to validate its legal-document and legal-advice features. The matter was finalized in February 2025: a $193,000 consumer-redress order, a requirement to notify past subscribers, and a forward-looking prohibition on claiming the service performs at the level of a human lawyer without competent and reliable evidence. This was a federal consumer-protection/deceptive-advertising theory, not a state UPL charge — and it applies regardless of which state a company operates in, so it sits alongside everything Texas-specific above rather than being superseded by it. Two direct implications for this product:

- Marketing copy, the go-to-market plan, and the product's own onboarding/disclosure language should avoid "AI lawyer," "robot lawyer," or equivalent framing that implies the tool substitutes for an attorney's judgment, and should be explicit that the tool performs intake and triage, with a human attorney making all substantive decisions.
- If any future claim is made about the tool's accuracy, quality, or capability, it should be substantiated before it appears in customer-facing materials — not asserted on the strength of the underlying model's general capability.

## 7. Design guardrails for the product (Texas)

Translating the above into concrete constraints for the cards already in flight or upcoming (`c12` conversational intake flow, `c13` LLM triage classifier, and anything downstream that generates client-facing text), specifically for the Texas version of the product:

- **Display the § 81.101(c) disclosure, clearly and conspicuously, in every conversation.** Since the software/forms exception is conditioned on a "clear and conspicuous" statement that the product isn't a substitute for an attorney's advice, this isn't just good practice — it's the specific statutory condition Texas attaches to the safe harbor this product most plausibly relies on. It should appear before or at the very start of intake, not buried in a terms-of-service link.
- **Collect facts; don't characterize them.** The intake flow should ask questions and record answers without ever telling the prospective client what those answers mean for their legal position ("that sounds like it's past the statute of limitations," "you have a strong claim," "this isn't worth pursuing"). This both avoids the general legal-advice line and keeps the product closer to the "software/forms" side of the § 81.101(c) exception rather than the "rendering of a service requiring legal skill" side of § 81.101(a).
- **Route, don't decide.** The triage classifier's output ("personal injury, high urgency") should be treated internally as a queue-sorting signal for firm staff, not surfaced to the prospective client as a legal conclusion about their matter.
- **No drafting legal documents from a client's answers**, at least not without a licensed attorney reviewing and approving the output before it reaches the client.
- **No outbound solicitation without a dedicated barratry review**, especially anything touching personal-injury or wrongful-death matters — the 31-day rule and the broader Penal Code § 38.12 restrictions in Section 4 apply regardless of whether a human or the product initiates contact. Keep the product's Texas deployment purely inbound (client-initiated) until and unless outbound features get their own legal review.
- **Stay firm-scoped, not a multi-firm referral service, in Texas specifically** — Section 5's Chapter 952 nonprofit/governmental eligibility requirement makes a for-profit routing-across-firms model a non-starter under current Texas law without a separate corporate structure. If go-to-market plans ever move that direction for Texas, that's a decision (and a structural change) for Clayton and counsel, not a feature to ship incrementally.
- **No "you don't need a lawyer" outputs, ever**, and no output that discourages someone from seeking counsel.

## 8. Open questions for attorney review

- Whether the § 81.101(c) software/forms exception, written in 1999 for static document-assembly software, plausibly extends to a conversational, LLM-driven intake flow that adapts its questions in real time — or whether that level of interactivity risks being treated as "the rendering of a service requiring legal skill or knowledge" under § 81.101(a) instead. This is the single biggest open legal question in this document and should be the first thing counsel weighs in on.
- Whether the specific disclosure language this product plans to use satisfies "clear and conspicuous" as Texas courts or the UPLC would read it — there's no bright-line test identified in this research pass for what satisfies that standard in an AI chat context specifically.
- Whether any planned product feature (e.g., automated follow-up messages, retargeting, "your inquiry has been received, here's what happens next" sequences) could be read as "solicitation" under Penal Code § 38.12 even when the initial contact was client-initiated — the barratry statute's boundaries around ongoing/automated communication with an existing inbound lead haven't been mapped in this pass.
- Whether Texas Disciplinary Rule of Professional Conduct 5.5(b) (a lawyer may not assist a non-lawyer in UPL) creates any obligation the vendor should build into the deploying firm's onboarding or contract (`c10`) — e.g., requiring the firm's supervising attorney to review scripted language before it goes live.
- Whether contractual terms with deploying Texas firms (`c10`) should include an indemnification or acknowledgment that the firm, not the vendor, is responsible for supervising the tool's use and reviewing its output before it's relied on with a client.
- This document does not cover any state other than Texas. When `c28` identifies additional launch states, each will need its own review at this depth — none of Sections 3–5 above should be assumed to generalize, since the software exception, the specific barratry rule, and the referral-service licensing scheme are all Texas-specific statutory choices, not universal features of UPL law.
