## 1. Why this matters and what's actually at risk

The product's core function — a conversational AI that takes in a prospective client's facts, sorts them by practice area and urgency, and helps a firm decide whether to take the matter — sits directly on top of the line between "legal information" (generally fine for anyone to provide) and "legal advice" (restricted to licensed attorneys in essentially every U.S. state). Every state has some version of an unauthorized-practice-of-law (UPL) statute or common-law doctrine, enforcement ranges from bar-initiated cease-and-desist letters up to criminal misdemeanor charges in some states, and — as the FTC's 2024–2025 action against DoNotPay showed — UPL is no longer the only regulatory exposure. Marketing an AI tool's capabilities in a way regulators view as overstated can also trigger consumer-protection claims, independent of whether a state bar ever gets involved.

Two distinct risk surfaces exist for this product, and they should not be conflated:

1. **Risk to the deploying law firm.** If a firm licenses this product and its own staff/attorneys use it as an intake tool (the primary go-to-market model per `docs/product`), the firm is a licensed entity directing a tool the way it might direct a paralegal or an answering service. This is the lower-risk posture, analogous to existing legal-intake software (Clio Grow, Lawmatics, Intaker) and answering services (Smith.ai) that already operate in this space without having been found to constitute UPL, provided the tool doesn't independently generate legal advice or legal conclusions that the firm passes along unreviewed.
2. **Risk to us as the vendor, if the product (or a future version of it) talks to prospective clients in a way that itself looks like practicing law** — e.g., if the AI tells a user "you have a valid claim," "you're within the statute of limitations," "you don't need a lawyer for this," or drafts something like a demand letter or a specific legal document based on their facts. This is the DoNotPay scenario, and it is the scenario the product's language, scripting, and feature set need to be actively designed away from — not just at launch, but as a standing constraint on every future feature that touches user-facing text.

The rest of this document treats risk surface 2 as the one that needs concrete design guardrails, since risk surface 1 is largely a function of how the deploying firm supervises the tool (a contractual/terms-of-service matter — see the related `c10` Terms of Service card, which should cross-reference this document).

## 2. The general legal framework

There is no single national definition of "the practice of law." The ABA considered developing one and abandoned the effort in 2003, recommending each state set its own standard, and that remains the state of play today — every jurisdiction has its own statute, bar rule, or case law defining UPL, and they are not uniform. That said, a few things are broadly consistent across states surveyed below:

- **The core test is "legal advice" vs. "legal information."** Explaining what the law generally says, publishing self-help materials, and providing blank forms that a person fills out and interprets themselves is treated as legal information and is not UPL. Applying the law to a specific person's facts and circumstances — telling them what it means for *them* and what they should do — is legal advice, and providing it without a license is UPL in essentially every state reviewed.
- **Document assembly is a gray zone, not a safe harbor.** Software that merely reproduces blank forms is fine. Software that asks a series of questions and then selects, customizes, or organizes legal content in response to the answers has been challenged as UPL in several jurisdictions (the classic example is the long-running litigation and regulatory attention around products like LegalZoom and Rocket Lawyer in various states), because the selection and customization step looks like the application of legal judgment even when a human isn't doing it. An AI intake flow that dynamically branches its questions and characterizes what it learns (e.g., sorting a user into "strong personal injury claim" vs. "weak claim") is closer to this gray zone than a static form.
- **"Average person" standard.** Several states (Illinois among them) frame the test as whether the service requires "legal knowledge or skill" beyond what an average layperson has. Practice-area triage and urgency classification plainly requires applying legal-adjacent judgment (is this a tort, a contract dispute, a criminal matter, is it time-sensitive), which is why this product's classifier (`c13`) needs to be scoped carefully — see Section 5.
- **Referral services and intake sit in an explicitly regulated space.** Most states separately regulate "lawyer referral services" (who may operate one, what they may charge, what disclosures they must give) as distinct from general UPL rules. If this product's DTC-facing chat ever operates independently of a specific engaged firm (e.g., as a lead-routing layer across multiple firms), lawyer-referral-service rules — not just UPL — become directly relevant and should be scoped as a follow-on research item once the go-to-market model firms up.
- **Multijurisdictional exposure compounds the state-by-state problem.** A firm is licensed in specific states; an AI intake tool doesn't know where a prospective client's matter is legally centered until it asks, and "unauthorized practice" risk attaches based on where the client is or where the matter arises, not just where the firm is licensed. Model Rule of Professional Conduct 5.5 (adopted in some form in most states) restricts a lawyer from practicing in a jurisdiction where they aren't licensed; a multi-tenant intake product used by firms licensed in different states needs to be careful that it doesn't itself become the entity "practicing" across state lines through the same script regardless of the user's location.

## 3. State-by-state notes (representative sample)

Card `c28` ("Narrow target launch states," a Founder task) has not yet been decided, so a full 50-state survey isn't the right use of effort yet — the scope of a UPL review should track the actual launch states once chosen. In the meantime, this section reviews a representative sample spanning different regulatory postures (a large plaintiff-heavy state, a state with recent chatbot-specific legislation, two states with recent bar guidance on AI specifically, and one with an unusually broad statutory definition) so the product's early design decisions have *something* concrete to build against. **This section should be re-run and expanded once target states are chosen.**

**California.** Business & Professions Code §§ 6125–6126 prohibit practicing law in California without being an active State Bar licensee; violation can be charged as a misdemeanor or, on a second offense, a felony. California case law defines "practice of law" broadly (applying legal principles to a client's particular facts, whether or not before a tribunal), and California is also notable for having a licensed non-lawyer category — Legal Document Assistants (LDAs), regulated under Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 6400–6415 — who may prepare self-help legal documents at a customer's specific direction but may *not* give advice about how to answer the questions on those documents or which legal options to pick. The LDA framework is a useful reference model: it shows California already permits guided-but-non-advisory document assistance, so long as the guidance never crosses into telling the person what they should do or which option applies to them.

**New York.** UPL is addressed under Judiciary Law § 478 (unauthorized practice) and § 484 (limits on who may practice), and New York is currently the most active state on AI-specific legislation in this space: a 2026 bill (S.7263) would explicitly extend existing restrictions on unlicensed professional practice — including the Judiciary Law's UPL provisions — to AI chatbot operators, require a clear and conspicuous AI disclosure, bar the company from using "it's just an AI" as a liability shield, and create a private right of action letting an affected user sue the chatbot's operator directly for damages (with fee-shifting for intentional violations). This has not passed as of this writing, but it signals where regulatory attention is heading and is worth tracking as a live bill rather than filing away as background research. Separately, the New York City Bar's Formal Opinion 2024-5 addresses lawyers' own use of generative AI (competence, confidentiality, supervision) but is aimed at attorneys already using AI tools in their practice, not at a UPL analysis of the tool itself — relevant to how a deploying firm should govern its staff's use of this product, less so to the product's own UPL exposure.

**Texas.** The Texas Center for Legal Ethics' Opinion 705 (Feb. 2025), issued through the State Bar's Taskforce on Responsible AI in the Law, covers attorneys' ethical obligations when using generative AI (competence, verification of AI output, the *Mata v. Avianca* hallucinated-citations episode as a cautionary example) but is, again, guidance for licensed attorneys using AI — not a UPL ruling on AI tools themselves. Texas's Unauthorized Practice of Law Committee separately investigates and can pursue injunctive relief against non-lawyer UPL, and has historically been active against document-preparation services; no AI-specific UPL enforcement action was identified in this pass, but the Committee's general approach (document assembly + guidance = UPL risk; document assembly alone = generally permissible) tracks the national pattern in Section 2.

**Florida.** The Florida Bar's Ethics Opinion 24-1 addresses lawyers' use of generative AI and sets expectations around confidentiality of client information fed into AI tools, competent supervision of AI-assisted work, accurate billing (not billing client rates for AI-accelerated work as if it were fully attorney time), and compliance with advertising rules if AI-generated content is used in marketing. Florida has historically been one of the more aggressive states on UPL enforcement generally (particularly around real estate closings and immigration document services), which argues for a conservative design posture if Florida is a target launch state.

**Illinois.** The Illinois State Bar Association's UPL guidance defines the practice of law broadly — any advice or service "requiring legal knowledge or skill" — and explicitly states that non-lawyers may not guide someone through completing a legal form (only provide it for self-completion), may not act as independent paralegals outside attorney supervision, and flags family law, real estate, estate planning, and bankruptcy as the practice areas with the most active UPL enforcement. That practice-area list is directly relevant to how this product's triage classifier should be tuned if Illinois is a launch state: those categories likely warrant the most conservative, fact-gathering-only scripting.

## 4. The DoNotPay precedent (why this is not hypothetical)

In September 2024 the FTC brought an enforcement action against DoNotPay for marketing itself as "the world's first robot lawyer" and claiming its AI could generate legal documents "on par with the work of expensive attorneys," without ever testing the tool against actual licensed-attorney work or employing attorneys to validate its legal-document and legal-advice features. The matter was finalized in February 2025: a $193,000 consumer-redress order, a requirement to notify past subscribers, and a forward-looking prohibition on claiming the service performs at the level of a human lawyer without competent and reliable evidence. Notably, the FTC's action was a consumer-protection/deceptive-advertising theory, not a UPL charge — which is an important distinction: even if a product's underlying conduct never gets challenged by a state bar as UPL, *marketing it as a substitute for a lawyer* is an independent and apparently very live enforcement risk. This has two direct implications for this product:

- Marketing copy, the go-to-market plan (`docs/go-to-market`), and the product's own onboarding/disclosure language should avoid "AI lawyer," "robot lawyer," or equivalent framing that implies the tool substitutes for an attorney's judgment, and should be explicit that the tool performs intake and triage, with a human attorney making all substantive decisions.
- If any future claim is made about the tool's accuracy, quality, or capability, it should be substantiated (tested against real outcomes, or attorney-reviewed) before it appears in customer-facing materials — not asserted on the strength of the underlying model's general capability.

## 5. Design guardrails for the product

Translating the above into concrete constraints for the cards already in flight or upcoming (`c12` conversational intake flow, `c13` LLM triage classifier, and anything downstream that generates client-facing text):

- **Collect facts; don't characterize them.** The intake flow should ask questions and record answers without ever telling the prospective client what those answers mean for their legal position ("that sounds like it's past the statute of limitations," "you have a strong claim," "this isn't worth pursuing"). Those are attorney judgment calls, full stop, even when the underlying facts make the answer seem obvious.
- **Route, don't decide.** The triage classifier's output ("personal injury, high urgency") should be treated internally as a queue-sorting signal for firm staff, not surfaced to the prospective client as a legal conclusion about their matter. The classifier itself should be framed and documented (in `c13`'s design doc) as an operational routing tool, not a legal-merit assessment, and that framing should hold even when the model's confidence is high.
- **No drafting legal documents from a client's answers**, at least not without a licensed attorney reviewing and approving the output before it reaches the client — this is squarely the document-assembly gray zone described in Section 2, and squarely what the deploying firm's engagement-letter workflow (`c4`) should keep on the human side of the line, even as that feature gets automated.
- **Persistent, plain-language AI disclosure.** Every conversation should disclose, before or at the start of intake, that the prospective client is talking to an automated system, not an attorney, and that nothing said in the chat is legal advice — independent of whether a state like New York makes this a statutory requirement, it is cheap to implement and directly blunts both the UPL and deceptive-advertising exposure described above.
- **No "you don't need a lawyer" outputs, ever**, and no output that discourages someone from seeking counsel — this is one of the more consistently cited red flags across UPL cases nationally.
- **Firm-scoped, not consumer-facing-independent, by default.** Keep the product positioned as a tool a licensed firm uses to run its own intake (lower risk surface, Section 1) rather than a consumer-facing "get matched with a lawyer" product, unless and until lawyer-referral-service rules are separately researched — that's a materially different regulatory regime and shouldn't be entered into by accident as the product evolves.
- **Jurisdiction-aware scripting once target states are chosen.** Once `c28` narrows the launch states, the specific practice areas flagged as UPL enforcement hotspots in a given state (e.g., Illinois's family law/real estate/estate planning/bankruptcy list) should get extra scripting conservatism in that state's version of the flow.

## 6. Open questions for attorney review

- Whether the "firm-scoped tool" characterization actually holds up if the product's marketing or contract structure ever positions it as interacting with the public on the firm's behalf in a way courts might treat as the firm (or the vendor) directly answering inquiries, versus a pure intake/scheduling function.
- Whether state-specific lawyer-referral-service statutes apply to any future multi-firm routing model, and if so, in which states.
- Whether the LDA-style "guided but non-advisory" California framework offers a workable template to formalize into the flow's scripting rules, or whether it's specific enough to California's statutory scheme that it shouldn't be generalized to other states.
- Full review once `c28` (target launch states) is decided — this document currently covers five representative states, not the actual launch set.
- Whether contractual terms with deploying firms (`c10`) should include an indemnification or acknowledgment that the firm, not the vendor, is responsible for supervising the tool's use and reviewing its output before it's relied on with a client — standard practice for legal-tech vendors, but should be drafted with counsel.
